Switzerland has achieved an unprecedented milestone in Europe: it employs over 400,000 cross-border workers. To understand what this means in concrete terms, one need only look at the past. In 1978, following the economic crisis of 1974-75, there were only 84,000 cross-border workers. The leap in time — from just under 100,000 to four times as many — was not linear, but experienced a decisive acceleration after 2000, when the agreement on the free movement of persons between Switzerland and the European Union came into force. The robustness of the Swiss economy did the rest.

Operational details

Structural growth in 48 years

From 1978 to 2026, the number of cross-border workers in Switzerland has grown nearly 4.8 times. It is not a random growth, nor is it a consequence of massive immigration. Rather, it is the structural result of two factors: the economic integration that followed the free movement agreement of 2000, and the persistent development disparity between European economies. Switzerland, with its salaries and the quality of work, remains a powerful pole of attraction for those living on the other side of the border — especially in Ticino, where the borders with Italy allow a daily commute that is not always easy, but feasible.

The three areas that absorb over 80% of the cross-border workforce have diverse characteristics. French-speaking Switzerland (Vaud, Jura, Valais, Geneva) attracts mainly from border regions in France. Ticino, especially with the Mendrisio district, engages with Lombard provinces. The two Basel cantons (BS and BL) receive workers from Alsace, central France, and southwestern Germany. Each area has developed a cross-border economy with its own peculiarities and specific labor market dynamics.

The myth of the 'theft of jobs'

The question of whether cross-border workers 'steal jobs from residents' is complex. The book does not provide a simplistic answer. According to the authors, cross-border work is the result of globalization and unequal development in the capitalist system. For this reason, say Rossini and Rennwald, it will be possible to regulate it only through the construction of a social Switzerland and a social Europe — that is, through greater harmonization of salaries, labor rights, and social protection between countries.

Key points

What it means concretely for the cross-border worker

If you work daily across the border, the content of this book concerns you directly. It is not an abstract treatise on economic sociology, but a manual that answers 30 concrete questions about the aspects that affect your life: salaries, taxes, permits, social security, housing and mobility.

The growth from 84,000 to 400,000 cross-border workers in 48 years means that your status is no longer considered an anomaly, but a permanent reality of the Swiss economy. This has positive implications (greater regulatory recognition, more stable bilateral agreements) and persistent challenges (wage competition in low-skilled sectors, fiscal complexity in double taxation). The book analyzes how these phenomena manifest in the three main areas — including Ticino — and what role public policies play in regulating them.

Frequently Asked Questions
How many border workers work in Switzerland in 2026?
Over 400,000 border workers work in Switzerland, the highest number in Europe. In 1978 there were 84,000. Growth was boosted by the free movement agreement that came into force in 2000 and the robustness of the Swiss economy.
Do frontier workers really steal jobs from Swiss residents?
According to the book, cross-border work is a structural consequence of globalization and unequal development in capitalism. It is not a matter of 'job theft', but of equal regulation. Today, xenophobic reflexes are less marked than in the past, although traffic at the crossings remains a source of tension.
What are the three areas that concentrate 80% of the border workforce?
French Switzerland (Vaud, Valais, Jura, Geneva), Ticino and the two cantons of Basel. Each has specific economic characteristics and different relationships with neighbouring regions (France, Italy, Germany).
What is covered in the new book 'Le travail frontier en Suisse'?
The book answers 30 questions on the border phenomenon: employment, wages, social dumping, social security, taxation, training, housing, territorial planning, transport and teleworking. It is written by former national councillors Rossini and Rennwald, journalist Droz and geographer Moine.
What role does public transport play in the debate on border crossers?
Highway traffic is one of the main sources of friction between Swiss and border residents. Increasing the use of public transport could reduce traffic jams and social tensions, improving daily coexistence between local communities and commuters.
