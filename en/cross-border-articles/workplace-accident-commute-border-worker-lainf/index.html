What is a commuting injury for the cross-border worker

A cross-border worker who suffers an accident on his way to work in Switzerland benefits from full coverage by LAINF, the federal accident insurance. This right is guaranteed to all employees, regardless of where they reside: a worker who lives in Italy and crosses the border every morning towards the Canton of Ticino receives the same protection as someone residing in Switzerland. To carry out this activity, the cross-border worker has a G permit, which gives him full access to Swiss employment protection systems.

Commuting accident is specifically defined as a harmful event occurring during the ordinary and direct journey between home and the workplace. It is considered an integral part of the working day, not a private life event. This means that it is automatically recognized as a consequence of the Swiss employment relationship, without the need for additional demonstrations of its relationship with the professional activity.

Coverage includes any form of legitimate transportation: personal automobile, public transportation, bicycle, or walking. What really distinguishes a protected accident is the ordinary and direct route: significant diversions, extra-work activities during the journey, or unjustified alternative routes can compromise coverage.

How claim notification works

When a cross-border commuter suffers an accident during the journey, he does not have to contact the accident insurer directly. It is the employer who has the legal obligation to notify the incident within the established deadlines. The company has a contact person (usually the human resources office) who fills out the complaint form and sends it to the UVG insurance.

However, the cross-border worker must immediately report the accident to his employer, providing as much detail as possible: precise time, location, circumstances of the incident, any witnesses, license plate number of the vehicle if involved. It is also advisable to put this communication in writing (email, message) to create a documentary trail and certify the timeliness of the report.

Operational details

Commuting accident versus work trip: crucial distinctions

One of the most delicate issues for cross-border workers concerns the difference between a "commuting accident" (during the ordinary home-work journey) and an accident that occurs during a work trip. Although both can generate compensation rights, the legal framework is significantly different and the cross-border worker must understand this to protect himself.

Commuting accident:

  • It is necessary during the ordinary and direct journey between home and office
  • It is an automatic part of the working day
  • It is covered by LAINF without the need for an explicit order from the employer
  • Protection starts from the moment of departure from home until arrival at work

Work trip:

  • The accident occurs in a temporary workplace, different from the usual one
  • It is preceded by an explicit mandate or request from the employer to go to another location
  • The coverage remains UVG, but the protected period is wider: from departure from home to return, even if multi-day or with an overnight stay
  • The documentation of the trip (service order, travel tickets, receipts) is important

For the Ticino cross-border worker, this distinction is crucial in practice. An employee who suffers an injury during the ordinary journey to Lugano, Mendrisio or Bellinzona is automatically covered and does not have to do anything extraordinary beyond notifying the employer. If, however, he suffers an accident during an authorized trip to another Swiss region - for example a conference in Zurich or a meeting in Geneva - the coverage remains identical, but the documentary context is different and the employer must explicitly report that the accident occurred during a business trip.

Alternative routes and detours: what remains covered

A frequent question among cross-border commuters concerns deviations from the usual route. If an employee changes his route for traffic reasons, to accompany a colleague who lives on the road, or for a short errand, is the accident covered by UVG?

The answer is conditional: small deviations from the ordinary path are automatically covered. An example would be taking a detour due to a non-working traffic light, parking far from the office for availability reasons, or taking an alternative route due to an accident that blocks the usual route. These details are considered part of the 'ordinary route' and do not exclude cover.

However, significant diversions — such as running a long personal errand, accompanying a friend in a direction other than your place of work, or engaging in a business en route — may disqualify you from protection. The reason is that they transform the journey from a "journey to work" into a "private life journey with incidental work elements". In these cases, the insurance may refuse to recognize the accident as a consequence of the employment relationship.

The cross-border worker must therefore remain aware that the journey must remain, in its essence, primarily oriented towards going to work. Minimal and reasonable detours remain covered; Substantial and deliberate deviations may irrevocably compromise the right to UVG coverage.

Accident reporting procedure: step-by-step guide

When an accident occurs while commuting, the cross-border worker must follow a precise procedure so that the claim is processed correctly and the rights are protected without delay.

Step 1 — Immediate notification to the employer Immediately after the accident, the cross-border worker must communicate what happened to the employer (or the company manager present). This oral communication is sufficient in the moment, but it is also recommended to put it in writing — an email, a WhatsApp message, or a dated note. This documentation must include: precise date, time, location of the accident, how it happened, any witnesses, and their names and contact details. Don't wait days: Immediacy is crucial for legal documentation.

Step 2 — Employer initiates the official complaint The employer has the legal obligation to report the accident to the LAINF insurance within the legal deadlines (generally a few days). It is not the employee's role to make this communication directly. The company has a contact person, usually the human resources office, who fills out the official complaint form and sends it to the insurer. The cross-border worker can ask the employer for written confirmation that the complaint has been forwarded.

Step 3 — Collection of supporting documentation The cross-border worker must collect and keep:

  • Medical report from the emergency room or treatment facility (if necessary)
  • Digital photographs of the accident site (e.g. damage to the vehicle, road conditions at the time)
  • Full names and telephone numbers of witnesses, if any
  • Copy of any written communication sent to the employer
  • Receipts for advance out-of-pocket medical expenses (if applicable)

This documentation supports the claim and significantly speeds up the processing of the case by the insurer.

Step 4 — Medical treatment and reimbursement Once the accident has been reported to LAINF, all necessary medical treatment is covered: emergency room, specialist visits, physiotherapy, prescribed drugs, any surgical interventions. The cross-border worker does not have to pay any costs in advance; the healthcare provider (doctor, hospital, pharmacy) bills the insurance company directly. If the cross-border worker pays out of his own pocket, he must keep the receipts and request reimbursement from the insurer, attaching a copy of the medical documentation.

Step 5 — Temporary Disability Compensation and Annuity If the accident causes a period of inability to work (temporary disability), LAINF provides a daily allowance until you return to work. If the consequences are permanent and reduce the cross-border worker's earning capacity, the insurance provides a disability pension to compensate for the lost income.

Essential documents and timeline

To speed up the processing of the application, the cross-border worker should have at hand:

  • Copy of the communication to the employer with date and time
  • Initial medical certificate from the hospital or clinic
  • Report form completed by LAINF insurance (provided by the employer or available online)
  • Photographs of the accident scene
  • Names, surnames and telephone numbers of witnesses
  • Receipts for medical care and medications

Timing is critical: communication to the employer must take place on the same day or at most the following day. The written request to the employer in the following days (to confirm that the complaint has been forwarded) is a prudential best practice.

Where to look for support and assistance

A cross-border worker who has doubts about the procedure can contact his employer, who has the obligation to guide him and provide clarifications. If uncertainties persist regarding coverage, the calculation of the allowance, or the rights, the cross-border worker can contact the territorial offices of the SECO (State Secretariat for the Economy) or request advice from a trade union organisation, if registered.

Frequently Asked Questions
What exactly is an itinerary injury?
It is an accident that occurs during the ordinary and direct route between home and work. It is considered part of the working day and is automatically covered by LAINF (Federal Accident Insurance Act) without the need for explicit orders from the employer.
If I change my usual route for traffic, do I stay covered?
Yes, small detours (for a traffic light, alternate parking, or to avoid clogging) remain covered. They are considered part of the ordinary journey. However, significant deviations for personal commissions may exclude LAINF coverage.
Should I contact the accident insurance directly?
No. The employer has a legal obligation to notify LAINF of the accident within the statutory time limits. You only need to inform the employer immediately of what happened, providing precise details about the place, time, circumstances and witnesses.
What care is covered after an injury on itineraries?
All necessary medical care: emergency room, specialist visits, physiotherapy, prescribed drugs, surgeries. The healthcare provider invoices LAINF directly. If you pay out of your own pocket, keep receipts and request reimbursement from the insurer.
If I stay off work for weeks, do I get an allowance?
Yes. LAINF provides a daily allowance for the period of inability to work (temporary disability). If the consequences are permanent, the insurance provides a disability pension to compensate for the loss of earning capacity.
