Today, on social media, young people politicize everyday life situations. But what are the commonplaces of the right and left that reflect today's society? We tried to ask the young people.

'What is the right, what is the left' sang Giorgio Gaber in 1994. A satirical reflection on the stereotyped daily actions of right and left ideologies. Differences yes, but reducible to appearances, commonplaces. 30 years later, political polarization is back in vogue and becomes a topic. The Tager Anzeiger outlines it, analyzing the phenomenon. Oat milk is left, sparkling water is right, headlines the newspaper referring to a trend increasingly in vogue among young people.

'We take it out on history, but it's our fault,' Gaber always sang. We are the ones who attribute meaning to actions. So does Gen Z. The theme is complex, articulated. Through social media, ideologies are summarized in a few macro-topics. From the use of gasoline cars to the consumption of meat. Ideology, despite everything, I still believe it exists, said Mr. G. But is it still the case today?

In an ironic key, without even remotely trying to copy the singer-songwriter, we tried to ask the young people. The clichés for today's youth 'The afternoon at the lake kitesurfing is right, the barbecue, always at the lake, is left,' says a boy. 'Drums are left, electronic cigarettes are right,' comments a girl instead. Another university student, on the other hand, refers to clothing and states that 'colored clothes and canvas bags are left, while shirts are right.' 'Technology is right and nature is left,' another young person says, citing U.S. politics.

More seriously, another tells us that 'machismo is right and homosexuality is left.' A series of students then joke referring to 'long nails and extensions' as right-wing symbolism, while 'normal nails and colored hair' as left-wing elements. 'Horse riding is right, climbing is left,' continues another student. Finally, a group of 4 girls talks about free time: 'Right-wing is the disco, the concert at Tassino is left' and 'playing cards is left, poker is right.' Clichés, in fact. And Gaber knew it well.

Analysis of political clichés

Young people in Ticino show a clear tendency to categorize daily habits based on political ideologies. While this phenomenon may seem superficial, it reflects a broader trend of political polarization among the youth. The politicization of daily actions such as consuming certain foods or practicing specific sports may seem ironic, but it also reveals how political ideologies deeply influence daily life.

These political clichés can have significant implications for Ticino society. However, it's important to remember that these clichés are often exaggerated and do not necessarily reflect the complex reality of political ideologies.
