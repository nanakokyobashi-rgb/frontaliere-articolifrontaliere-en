The cross-border worker boom in numbers

Switzerland has become the European country employing the largest number of cross-border workers. In 1978, following the 1974-75 economic crisis, there were 84,000. Today, they exceed 400,000. This jump was not linear, but underwent a decisive acceleration after 2000, the year the agreement on the free movement of persons between Switzerland and the European Union came into force — also favored by the robustness of the Swiss economy.

A new book, titled 'Le travail frontalier en Suisse – 30 questions' and published by Alphil editions, aims to clarify a continuously growing phenomenon: debunking myths and analyzing challenges and opportunities through statistical data and answers to key questions. In public debate, the phenomenon of cross-border workers is often at the center of controversy and misunderstanding.

Operational details

The three key areas: geographical concentration and reasons

The concentration of border workers in three specific areas — French Switzerland, Ticino, Basel — is no coincidence. These regions benefit from geographical proximity to neighbouring countries, a robust local economy, and a historical tradition of cross-border economic exchanges. Ticino, in particular, serves as a bridge to the Italian Lombard economy. French Switzerland overlooks France, while Basel borders France and Germany.

This geographical concentration creates challenges common to all three areas: infrastructural pressure on border crossings, traffic congestion, coordination needs between health and social security systems, and local debates on competition in labour markets.

Contemporary challenges: from social dumping to xenophobia

The book addresses issues crucial to the daily life of the border worker. A first area concerns wages and social dumping: are frontier workers really paid less than resident workers? To what extent does their inflow affect local Swiss wages?

Social security is another priority. How does health insurance, the AVS (old-age pension) and the second pillar (LPP) work for those who work in Switzerland but reside in Italy? These issues directly affect the protection of the border worker during their working life.

Traffic is a recurring theme in the public debate on border crossers.

Frequently Asked Questions
Why is the book "Le travail frontier en Suisse" important for frontier workers?
The book addresses issues directly relevant to your daily life: wages, social security (AVS, LPP, health insurance), taxation, transport, xenophobia and discrimination. It provides an overview of the phenomenon through statistical data and critical analysis, dispelling common myths and giving you a deep understanding of the collective challenges that characterise Swiss cross-border work.
What are the three geographical areas covered by the book?
The book focuses on the three areas that absorb more than 80% of the border workforce in Switzerland: French Switzerland (border with France), Ticino (border with Italy), and the two cantons of Basel (border with France and Germany). These areas are the most significant in terms of the number of border crossers and their economic and social impact.
Why has the number of border crossers grown so rapidly since 2000?
The main acceleration is due to the entry into force of the agreement on the free movement of persons between Switzerland and the European Union in 2000. This agreement has removed many administrative barriers to cross-border work, allowing for much smoother mobility. The robustness of the Swiss economy has also attracted workers from neighbouring countries, triggering steady growth from 84,000 frontier workers in 1978 to over 400,000 today.
Does the book address the issue of discrimination against border workers?
Yes. According to author Jean-Claude Rennwald, xenophobic reflexes towards frontiersmen seem less marked today than in the past. The book suggests that this negative perception could be further attenuated if the use of public transport increases, thus reducing the visibility of road congestion and commuter flows, one of the main sources of friction between communities.
