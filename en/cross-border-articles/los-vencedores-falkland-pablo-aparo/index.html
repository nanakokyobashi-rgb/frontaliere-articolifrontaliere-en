In ‘Los vencedores’, Pablo Aparo decides to go and tell the story of the Falklands from the point of view of its inhabitants, perhaps finding the key to a new beginning. The film was presented as part of the culture section of the Locarno Film Festival, as reported by the local source. The director's choice is to shift his gaze from traditional narratives about the Anglo-Argentine conflict towards the voices of those who live daily on the islands. This approach aims to explore not only the political and military consequences of conflict, but also the human, social and cultural experiences of local communities. The projection is part of a context of reflection on collective memories and the possibilities of reconciliation after periods of international tension. Operational details

Analysis of the cinematographic context

  • The Locarno Film Festival regularly hosts sections dedicated to commitment documentaries
  • The film is part of a global trend of historical reinterpretation from below
  • The choice of the point of view of the inhabitants is increasingly common in contemporary works

Pablo Aparo's decision to tell the Falklands through the eyes of the residents reflects a methodology that is increasingly widespread in international documentary cinema: starting from local memory to reconstruct events of global scope. This approach allows you to discover details often absent in history books, such as daily routines, perceptions of danger, forms of civil resistance or simply the way in which communities have continued to live despite the conflict. In the case of the Falklands, where the dominant narrative has long been military and diplomatic, giving voice to the inhabitants means highlighting aspects such as adapting to foreign military presence, managing limited resources, or maintaining isolated cultural identities. The film could therefore contribute to a more nuanced understanding of the conflict, not as a simple contrast between two states, but as an experience lived by civilian populations. This type of work is also linked to broader debates on the role of memory in post-conflict, on how societies process collective trauma and on which narratives are privileged over time.
