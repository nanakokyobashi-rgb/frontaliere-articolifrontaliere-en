Opening a VAT number as a cross-border worker in Switzerland requires an understanding of cross-border taxation, compulsory social security and insurance obligations. The New Cross-Border Agreement, applied from 1 January 2024, has introduced significant changes for those residing in Italy and working independently in Switzerland. The "old cross-border workers" fall under the art. 9 only if they satisfied the tax requirements and had worked in Graubünden, Ticino or Valais in the period indicated; for covered incomes they are taxed exclusively in Switzerland. Old cross-border workers (art. 9 of the Agreement) are taxed on their wages only in Switzerland; the IRPEF exemption of €10,000 is valid from 2024 for cross-border workers whose income is taxed in Italy. 4 L. 83/2023 concern only the income referred to in the art. 1, paragraph 175, Law 147/2013.

Self-employment vs. employed work for cross-border commuters

The decision to become self-employed in Switzerland entails administrative and fiscal responsibilities that are different from employment. A cross-border worker has withholding tax directly deducted from his salary by his Swiss employer; a self-employed person, on the other hand, must independently manage the obligations towards the Swiss and Italian authorities.

In the self-employment regime in Switzerland, the cross-border worker does not have an "employer" who pays the tax at source. However, the Convention between Italy and Switzerland of 9 March 1976, supplemented by the New Agreement of 23 December 2020 (effective 1 January 2024), establishes that withholding tax remains withheld in Switzerland. To avoid double taxation, Italy recognizes a tax credit (EC framework of 730) on those incomes.

Basic requirements to open a VAT number in Switzerland

For a cross-border worker who resides in Italy (Canton Ticino or border provinces) and wants to work independently in Switzerland, the following are necessary:

1. Valid G permit – The border permit allows self-employment in Switzerland for a maximum duration of five years, renewable. 2. Registration with the cantonal statistics office – Each Swiss canton requires the registration of an independent activity with the cantonal statistics office (USTAT, or equivalent body). 3. Swiss federal taxpayer number – Required to pay social security contributions (AVS, AI, IPG) and declare income with the Federal Tax Administration (AFC/ESTV). 4. Registration with the Italian INPS – To also manage the Italian social security position (if applicable for cross-border workers in the transitional regime).

The New Cross-border Workers Agreement distinguishes between old and new cross-border workers with regards to the self-employment income exemption. Old cross-border commuters remain taxed only in Switzerland (art. 9 of the 2020 Agreement). The threshold of 10,000 euros is not a general exemption: art. 4 of Law 83/2023 limits it to the income referred to in the art. 1, paragraph 175, of Law 147/2013. Beyond these amounts, the income is subject to the ordinary tax regime.

Operational details

Swiss taxation for self-employed cross-border commuters

For a self-employed cross-border worker, Swiss taxation is made up of three levels: federal, cantonal and municipal. Federal income tax is collected directly by the Federal Tax Administration (AFC/ESTV) and is calculated on a progressive basis. The Canton of Ticino applies additional cantonal and municipal rates, which vary depending on the municipality in which the activity is carried out.

The central element for the self-employed cross-border worker is the withholding tax. Unlike employed work (where the employer pays withholding tax on the salary), the self-employed must make quarterly or annual payments. However, Switzerland and Italy have agreed that this tax remains withheld in Switzerland. Italy recognizes the tax credit in the tax return (CE framework of the 730 declaration for cross-border workers who also declare in Italy). Double taxation agreement Italy-Switzerland

The Convention between Italy and Switzerland, signed on 9 March 1976, provides that income from self-employment carried out in Swiss territory is taxed by Switzerland (for new cross-border workers also in Italy, with tax credit). This means that:

1. Switzerland taxes the self-employed person's income (withholding tax + income tax) 2. Italy does NOT tax the same income again 3. The cross-border worker who resides in Italy and earns in Switzerland declares the Swiss income also in Italy, but requests the tax credit (EC framework of 730) for the taxes already paid in Switzerland

This avoids double taxation. However, it is essential that the cross-border commuter is correctly registered with the tax authorities of both countries.

Old cross-border workers vs. new cross-border workers: differences on the transitional regime

The New Cross-border Workers Agreement (23 December 2020, applied from 1 January 2024) distinguishes between old and new cross-border workers. Old cross-border workers remain taxed only in Switzerland pursuant to the art. 9 of the 2020 Agreement.

The threshold of 10,000 euros is not a general exemption: art. 4 of Law 83/2023 limits it to the income referred to in the art. 1, paragraph 175, of Law 147/2013. This difference reflects the principle of the New Agreement: encouraging the mobility of new cross-border workforce towards Switzerland, while protecting the minimum income of historic cross-border workers during the transitional period.

Beyond the exemption, self-employed income is subject to the Swiss withholding tax rate. The rate varies depending on the canton and level (federal, cantonal, municipal), but the regime remains the standard one for self-employed people in Switzerland.

Swiss social security and insurance contributions

A self-employed cross-border commuter registered with the Swiss authorities must pay contributions to Swiss social insurance:

  • AVS (Old age and survivors insurance): 5.3% of net income
  • AI (Disability Insurance): included in the 5.3% AVS
  • IPG (Indemnity for loss of earnings): included in the 5.3% AVS/AI
  • LPP (Pension Fund / second pillar): variable rate 7-18% depending on age (mandatory from the 25th year of age)
  • LAINF (Accident insurance): mandatory if income > CHF 9,300 per year; rate 0.7-1.5%

These contributions are paid directly by the self-employed cross-border worker, not by an employer. The calculation of net income must take into account all business expenses deductible according to Swiss legislation.

Key points

Health insurance (KVG) and supplementary pension

One of the lesser-known but crucial aspects for self-employed cross-border workers is health insurance (LAMal, or basic compulsory insurance). Switzerland requires all residents and employed to be enrolled in private health insurance. However, cross-border commuters (G Permit) have an option: they can remain registered with Italian insurance or transfer to a Swiss fund.

Many cross-border workers choose to remain in the Italian system (SSN), paying a contribution to INPS. Those who sign up for Swiss insurance must choose a private fund and pay the monthly premium directly. Swiss deductibles for adults range from CHF 300 to CHF 2,500 depending on the coverage model chosen.

For the third pillar (3a), the self-employed cross-border worker can pay voluntary supplementary pension contributions. The annual maximum is defined by Swiss federal legislation and depends on whether the self-employed person is registered with an LPP. See AVS and LPP guide to better understand your pension options.

Practical procedure: what to do step by step

Step 1: Check the status of cross-border worker and registration with the relevant Canton. Request the G Permit from the migration authorities of your Italian province of residence (questura or prefecture). The permit is valid for 5 years.

Step 2: Registration at the competent cantonal office. Once you have the G Permit, register your self-employed activity at the cantonal statistics office (USTAT or equivalent). Contact the canton office where you will carry out the activity directly.

Step 3: Obtain your federal taxpayer number and Swiss VAT identification number. The AFC/ESTV will assign a unique Federal Taxpayer Number (UID). If you make transactions with VAT, request the VAT-UID number from the same administration.

Step 4: Payment of social security contributions. Register with an AVS compensation organization for the payment of AVS/AI/IPG contributions. For the BVG, a registered cantonal pension fund will proceed with the collection.

Step 5: Health insurance. If you choose to remain in the Italian system, notify your INPS fund of the change in status (from employee to self-employed cross-border worker). If you opt for a Swiss fund, choose a private fund in the canton and sign up directly.

Step 6: Annual tax declaration in Switzerland. By the deadline set by the canton (usually the end of February or the end of March of the following year), submit the Swiss tax return to the AFC/ESTV. Attach documents of income and business expenses.

Step 7: Tax declaration in Italy. Even if you are self-employed in Switzerland, if you reside in Italy, you must declare your Swiss income to the Italian Revenue Agency (form 730 or PF income). In the CE framework, you request the tax credit for taxes paid in Switzerland.

Frequently Asked Questions
Can I open a VAT number in Switzerland if I live in Italy?
Yes, if you have a valid G (Border) Permit. You must register with the relevant cantonal office (USTAT or equivalent) and obtain a Swiss Federal Taxpayer Number (UID). Subsequently, you are subject to Swiss tax and social security obligations, while in Italy you declare income at the Revenue Agency and apply for the tax credit to avoid double taxation.
Which income does the €10,000 limit apply to?
Article 9’s transitional regime applies only to workers who meet the Agreement’s definition and worked in Graubünden, Ticino or Valais between 31 December 2018 and 17 July 2023; covered employment income is taxable exclusively in Switzerland. The ordinary regime applies in other cases, with Italian taxation and credit for Swiss tax where applicable. The €10,000 in Article 4 of Law 83/2023 is only the limit for income referred to in Article 1(175) of Law 147/2013, not a general allowance. FTA factsheet · Agreement, Article 9 · Normattiva, Article 4.
Who pays the tax at source if I am self-employed?
According to the Italian-Swiss Convention (9 March 1976) and the New Frontier Agreement, the tax at source is withheld exclusively from Switzerland. There is no double taxation: Italy recognizes a tax credit (EC framework of 730) on amounts already taxed in Switzerland. The freelancer pays directly to the AFC/ESTV, not to an employer.
What are the minimum contributions I have to pay as a self-employed person in Switzerland?
AVS/AI/IPG: 5.3% of net income. LPP (mandatory from the 25th year): 7-18% according to age group. LAINF: 0.7-1.5% if income > CHF 9,300 per annum. The amounts are paid directly to the social security institution or pension fund, not through an employer.
Do I have to be in the Italian or Swiss health system?
As a border worker (G Permit), you have the right of option: you can stay in the Italian NHS or register with a Swiss sickness fund (LAMal). If you stay in Italy, you pay contributions to INPS. If you switch to the Swiss system, choose a private cashier and pay the monthly premium. Swiss deductibles range from CHF 300 to CHF 2,500 for adults.
