Complete Guide to Becoming a Cross-Border Worker in Switzerland 2026

Di Laura Bianchi · Published on 3 April 2026 · Updated on 25 September 2026

From job search to first day: permits, taxes, pensions, insurance, and everything you need to work in Switzerland from Italy.

Context

A cross-border worker (in Italian frontaliere, in German Grenzgänger, in French frontalier) is a professional who resides in one country — typically Italy — and commutes daily or weekly to a neighbouring country — Switzerland — to carry out their work. In Ticino, this phenomenon involves over 79,000 people and represents a pillar of the regional economy, with fiscal, social security and logistical implications that require careful planning.

This guide covers every aspect of the cross-border worker journey: from the initial decision to daily life, including permits, taxation, pensions and health insurance. It is designed both for those evaluating whether to become a cross-border worker and for those who already are and want to optimise their situation.

A quick simulation

Consider a typical case: gross salary CHF 5,500/month, single person with no children, resident in Como.

With G Permit (cross-border worker):

  • Swiss social contributions (AVS, AC, LPP): ~CHF 660/month
  • Ticino withholding tax (table A): ~CHF 440/month
  • Net monthly in CH: ~CHF 4,400
  • Italian IRPEF (with €10,000 exemption): ~€280/month

With B Permit (resident in Ticino):

  • Swiss social contributions: ~CHF 660/month
  • Withholding tax: ~CHF 440/month
  • Net monthly: ~CHF 4,400
  • Studio apartment rent in Lugano: ~CHF 1,200/month
  • LAMal: ~CHF 380/month
  • Net after fixed costs: ~CHF 2,820/month (~€2,650)

The Swiss employment contract

Swiss contracts differ from Italian ones in several key aspects:

  • Probation period: 1-3 months (can be terminated with 7 days' notice)
  • Annual leave: minimum 4 weeks/year (5 for those under 20)
  • Notice period: 1 month in 1st year, 2 months from 2nd to 9th year, 3 months from 10th year
  • 13th month salary: not legally mandatory, but widespread (present in 90% of contracts in Ticino)
  • Working hours: typically 40-42 hours per week

Chapter 3: G Permit — How to obtain it

The G Permit (cross-border worker permit) is the document authorising an EU/EFTA citizen residing in a neighbouring country to work in Switzerland. Since 2002, thanks to the bilateral CH-EU agreements, the procedure is relatively straightforward for Italian citizens.

Requirements

1. EU/EFTA citizenship (or permanent residence permit in a neighbouring EU state) 2. Employment contract with a Swiss employer (or job offer) 3. Residence in the border zone: within a defined area from the border (traditionally 20 km, though the 2020 New Agreement has evolved this definition) 4. Weekly return: at least once per week to the main residence in Italy

Required documents

  • Valid passport or identity card
  • Employment contract or letter of offer
  • Italian certificate of residence
  • 1 passport-size photo
  • Permit application form (usually provided by the employer)

Renewal and loss of permit

The G Permit is automatically renewed every 5 years, provided employment continues. In case of job loss:

  • Less than 1 year of employment: the permit is revoked after 6 months of unemployment
  • More than 1 year of employment: right to remain registered as a cross-border worker for 6 months (SECO unemployment benefits)
  • More than 5 years of employment: extended right to 12 months

Chapter 4: Taxation — The heart of the matter

Taxation is the most complex and most important aspect for a cross-border worker. Since 2023, the tax regime has changed radically with the entry into force of the New Italy-Switzerland Agreement on cross-border worker taxation.

The old regime (workers pre-July 2023)

Those who started working as cross-border workers before 17 July 2023 benefit from a transitional regime lasting until 2033:

  • Swiss withholding tax only (no Italian taxation)
  • The withholding tax is deducted by the employer
  • 40% of the withholding tax is transferred back to Italian border municipalities (the so-called ristorni)
  • No obligation to file an Italian tax return for Swiss employment income

The 2020 New Agreement (workers from 17 July 2023)

For new cross-border workers (hired from 17 July 2023), the regime is radically different:

Table 5: Element
ElementDetail
Swiss withholding taxDeducted by employer, progressive rate by canton
Italian taxationSwiss income must be declared in Italy
Exemption€10,000 deduction to avoid double taxation
Tax creditTax paid in CH is deducted from Italian IRPEF
RistorniAbolished for new cross-border workers

Ticino withholding tax: tables A, B, C, H

The cantonal withholding tax is applied based on marital status and family situation:

  • Table A: Single person without children
  • Table B: Married, spouse not working or lower income
  • Table C: Married, both spouses working
  • Table H: Single parent

The rate is progressive and ranges from 2.5% (low incomes) to over 30% (incomes above CHF 15,000/month). It includes federal, cantonal and municipal tax.

Italian tax return

For new cross-border workers, the tax return becomes mandatory:

1. Convert income from CHF to EUR at the annual average rate (published by Agenzia delle Entrate) 2. Apply the €10,000 exemption (deduction from taxable income) 3. Calculate IRPEF on 2026 Italian brackets (23% up to €28,000, 33% up to €50,000, 43% above) 4. Deduct the tax credit for taxes paid in Switzerland 5. Add regional and municipal surcharges

"With the New Agreement, tax planning becomes fundamental. A cross-border worker who doesn't optimise their situation can pay up to 15% more in taxes compared to someone who plans properly. The key is understanding the interaction between the exemption, tax credit and Italian deductions." — Dr Elena Colombo, Tax Accountant specialising in international taxation

Chapter 5: Social security — AVS, LPP and 3rd pillar

The Swiss pension system is structured across three pillars and offers one of the most robust social safety nets in the world. As a cross-border worker, you contribute compulsorily to the first two and can voluntarily access the third.

1st Pillar: AVS/AI (Old Age and Survivors' Insurance)

The AVS is the state basic pension, comparable to Italy's INPS:

Table 6: Parameter
ParameterDetail
Employee contribution5.3% of gross salary
Employer contribution5.3% of gross salary
Maximum pension (2026)CHF 2,520/month (single), CHF 3,780/month (couple)
Retirement age65 years (men and women from 2028)
Years for full pension44 years

Coordination with INPS: Thanks to the bilateral CH-EU agreements, contribution periods in Switzerland and Italy are combined to meet minimum pension requirements in both countries. A cross-border worker who works 20 years in Switzerland and 15 in Italy will receive two separate pensions, each proportional to contributions paid.

2nd Pillar: LPP (Occupational Pension)

The LPP is the mandatory occupational pension, comparable to Italian TFR/pension funds:

Table 7: Age bracket
Age bracketTotal contribution (% of coordinated salary)Employee share
25-34 years7%3.5%
35-44 years10%5%
45-54 years15%7.5%
55-65 years18%9%

The coordinated salary is the portion between CHF 26,460 and CHF 90,720 (2026 thresholds). Accumulated 2nd pillar capital is significant: after 25 years of work with a salary of CHF 6,000/month, the LPP capital can exceed CHF 250,000.

"The Swiss 2nd pillar is probably the greatest long-term financial advantage for a cross-border worker. The problem is that many ignore it until retirement. I advise checking your pension fund statement at least once a year and considering voluntary buy-ins, which are tax-deductible." — Dr Andrea Fiorini, CH-IT Pension Consultant

3rd Pillar: individual pension provision

The 3rd pillar is voluntary and offers significant tax advantages:

  • Pillar 3a (tied): maximum contribution CHF 7,258/year (2026) for employed persons with LPP. Fully deductible from withholding tax.
  • Pillar 3b (flexible): no limit, limited tax advantages.

Note for cross-border workers: the right to Pillar 3a has been confirmed for cross-border workers, but requires that income is subject to Swiss withholding tax. The deduction is applied directly on the payslip.

Chapter 6: Health insurance — LAMal or Italian NHS?

Within 3 months of starting employment in Switzerland, the cross-border worker must make a crucial and (almost) irreversible choice: join the Swiss health system (LAMal) or exercise the right of option to remain in the Italian National Health Service (SSN).

The right of option

Italy negotiated with Switzerland the right for cross-border workers to choose where to insure themselves. This choice:

  • Must be communicated within 3 months of starting work
  • Is final for the duration of employment with the same employer
  • Applies to the worker and dependent family members

The CHF/EUR exchange rate: the other variable

The exchange rate directly impacts a cross-border worker's purchasing power. With a net salary of CHF 5,000:

  • At 0.90 EUR/CHF: you receive €4,500
  • At 0.95 EUR/CHF: you receive €4,750 (+€250/month)
  • At 1.00 EUR/CHF: you receive €5,000 (+€500/month)

Strategies to optimise the exchange:

1. Don't convert your entire salary at once: keep some in CHF for Swiss expenses 2. Use specialised services: Wise, Revolut and cross-border banks offer better rates than branch exchange counters 3. Monitor the rate: set alerts to exchange when the rate is favourable 4. Dollar-cost averaging: convert fixed amounts at regular intervals to average out fluctuations

Frequently asked questions (FAQ)

1. Can I become a cross-border worker if I live in Milan?

It depends on the regime. Under the old regime (pre-July 2023), the border zone was limited to 20 km from the border. Under the New Agreement, the definition is more flexible but still requires a weekly return. Milan (80 km from the border) is technically outside the traditional border zone, but with a contract from 2023 onwards and the new regulations, you may still qualify as a "cross-border worker" for tax purposes if you commute daily. Consult a tax advisor for your specific case.

2. How much does a cross-border worker earn on average?

The median salary for cross-border workers in Ticino is CHF 5,400/month gross (FSO, 2025). However, the range is wide: from CHF 3,800 for retail positions to CHF 9,000+ for specialised IT and finance roles. Use the salary comparison to see data for your sector.

3. Does the €10,000 exemption apply to everyone?

No. The €10,000 exemption applies only to new cross-border workers (hired from 17 July 2023 onwards). Workers under the old regime do not pay IRPEF in Italy, so the exemption is not relevant for them.

4. Can I deduct commuting expenses?

In Switzerland: yes, transport costs can be deducted from withholding tax (up to CHF 3,200/year for public transport; for car commutes, it's calculated by km). The deduction must be claimed through a withholding tax correction. In Italy: commuting expenses are not directly deductible from IRPEF, but they factor into the overall financial calculation.

5. What happens to my LPP if I return to Italy permanently?

If you leave Switzerland for an EU country, you have two options for the 2nd pillar:

  • Leave the capital in a Swiss vested benefits account until retirement age
  • Withdraw the supra-mandatory portion in cash (the mandatory portion stays in Switzerland if the destination country has an equivalent pension system — Italy does)

Since 2025, case law has clarified that mandatory LPP capital cannot be withdrawn in cash when returning to an EU country with an equivalent pension system. Plan ahead with the pension planner.

6. LAMal or NHS: can I change my mind afterwards?

In principle, no. The choice made within 3 months of starting employment is final for the duration of the contract with the same employer. However, a change of employer (even brief) can technically reopen the right of option. It's a grey area: check our health insurance guide.

7. Will the ristorni be abolished?

For workers under the old regime, the ristorni (40% withholding tax transfer to Italian border municipalities) are guaranteed until 2033, after which they will be gradually reduced. For new cross-border workers (from July 2023), ristorni do not apply as the tax mechanism is different (double taxation with credit). Track changes with our ristorni tracker.

8. How long does it take to get a G Permit?

With a signed employment contract, the G Permit is issued in 2-4 weeks. The employer generally handles the application. At certain times of year (September, January) processing times may be longer. The permit is valid for 5 years and renews automatically.

Conclusion: the cross-border worker as a binational professional

Being a cross-border worker in 2026 is not simply "working in Switzerland and living in Italy." It is a life choice that requires financial planning skills, regulatory knowledge and daily organisation that few other workers need to possess.

The modern cross-border worker is a binational professional navigating between two tax systems, two pension regimes, two healthcare systems and two markets. This complexity, when managed well, translates into a significant competitive advantage: Swiss salaries with Italian cost of living, one of the world's most robust pension systems, and access to two labour markets.

"The cross-border worker of the future is no longer the passive commuter of the 1980s. They are a conscious professional who strategically chooses where to live, where to work, where to pay taxes and where to build their pension. Those who understand this complexity and actively manage it have an enormous advantage over those who simply accept the system as it is." — Prof. Roberto Bentivoglio, Professor of Labour Law, USI

Frequently Asked Questions
How many Italian border workers work in Ticino and what impact do they have on the economy?
There are 79,207 Italian border workers in Ticino (Q4 2025), representing 28.3% of the cantonal workforce. This phenomenon is an economic pillar, with a growth of 15.2% in the last 5 years, driven by the wage gap and geographical proximity. The manufacturing sector is the most represented with 31%.
What is the median salary for a border worker in Ticino?
The median wage for frontier workers in Ticino is CHF 5,400 per month, according to the Swiss survey of the wage structure. This value is significantly higher than Italian salaries, with a ratio of about 2.3:1 for equivalent positions, making cross-border work economically advantageous.
What are the main differences between Permit G (border) and Permit B (resident in Switzerland)?
Permit G provides for residence in Italy and mixed taxation (Switzerland + Italian personal income tax), with Italian living costs and daily commuting. Permit B implies residence in Switzerland, Swiss taxation only, Swiss living costs and no or short commuting. Health insurance also differs: choice for G, mandatory LAMal for B.
Is it economically convenient to become a border worker with a G Permit compared to residing in Switzerland with a B Permit?
For a single person with a gross salary of CHF 5.500/mese, a border worker with a G Permit residing in Como can obtain a final net of approximately € 3.700/mese. A resident in Ticino with Permit B, with the same salary, after rent and LAMal, would have about € 2.650/mese. The advantage of the border crossing can vary between 500 and 1,200 euros per month, but the costs of commuting reduce it.
What are the job sectors with the greatest opportunities for border workers in Ticino in 2025?
The sectors with the most opportunities in Ticino for 2025-2026 are IT (22% of offers, CHF 7.200/mese), Manufacturing (18%, CHF 5.100/mese) and Healthcare and Pharma (14%, CHF 6.800/mese). Construction and construction also shows a growing trend.
