Roche focuses on next-generation drugs

In early March 2026, pharmaceutical giant Roche announced preliminary positive results for Petrelintide, an anti-obesity drug developed in collaboration with Danish biotech company Zealand Pharma. Phase 2 trials involved patients receiving a weekly injection for 42 weeks. The results showed an average weight loss of 10.7%, with mild side effects. However, the data failed to meet investor expectations: Zealand Pharma’s shares plummeted by 35%, while Roche lost around 3% the day after the announcement.

According to Manu Chakravarthy, Roche’s Global Head of Cardiovascular, Renal and Metabolic Development, the goal is not solely about weight loss. 'We’re not just looking at the number on the scale. What truly matters is the person’s overall health,' he stated. 'Not everyone will need the same thing at the same time in their obesity journey, which can span 20, 40, or even 60 years, especially when considering associated comorbidities.'

📊 A second wave of targeted therapies

Roche missed the first wave of GLP-1-based drugs’ success (such as Novo Nordisk’s Wegovy and Eli Lilly’s Zepbound), which deliver weight loss between 15% and 20%, with peaks exceeding 25% in some studies. Now, the company is investing billions in a new generation of therapies, focusing on more personalized and targeted drugs. Petrelintide is just one of Roche’s strategies: the company has struck deals with several biotech firms to expand its portfolio in an increasingly competitive market.

The global anti-obesity drug market is expanding rapidly. According to IQVIA, tirzepatide (Zepbound/Mounjaro) sales surpassed $36.5 billion in 2025, becoming the world’s best-selling drug. Market estimates project growth from $66 billion in 2025 to $100–200 billion by 2030. However, current penetration of these drugs remains below 10% of the global obese population, particularly in the United States.

⚠️ Obesity as a global health issue

The World Health Organization (WHO) defines obesity as a chronic disease characterized by excessive fat accumulation with significant health risks. Measurement is based on Body Mass Index (BMI), calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m²). A BMI above 25 indicates overweight, while a value of 30 or higher is classified as obesity. Thresholds may vary depending on ethnic groups.

GLP-1-based drugs are generally approved for adults with a BMI of 30 or higher, or a BMI of 27 or higher in the presence of related conditions such as hypertension, type 2 diabetes, or high cholesterol. According to a study published in The Lancet, by 2050 nearly 60% of people over 25 will be overweight or obese, up from 29% in 1990. The sharpest increase is occurring in sub-Saharan Africa.

Global obesity-related costs could reach $3,000 billion (2,380 billion francs) annually by 2030, according to the World Obesity Federation, exceeding $18,000 billion by 2060. This represents a massive commercial opportunity for pharmaceutical companies, but also a challenge for national healthcare systems.

However, the availability of alternatives like Petrelintide could accelerate uptake, particularly in cross-border contexts where patients have access to both the Swiss and Italian healthcare systems.

Operational details

Why Roche Missed the First Wave of GLP-1 Drugs

In 2010, Roche was among the first companies to study GLP-1 drugs, but abandoned the project after a series of late-stage failures, just before Novo Nordisk and Eli Lilly's success boom. "We missed the first wave," Chakravarthy admitted. Now, with the introduction of Petrelintide and other targeted therapies, the company is trying to catch up in a market that is worth tens of billions and is growing at a rapid pace.

Roche's strategy is based on three pillars: personalization, reduction of side effects and a holistic approach to patient health. While drugs such as Wegovy and Zepbound focus on weight loss, Roche focuses on solutions that also improve metabolic, cardiovascular and renal parameters. This approach could attract patients with obesity and comorbidities, a target often overlooked by current therapies.

Risks for cross-border commuters: costs and accessibility

For cross-border commuters who live in Ticino but work in Lombardy, access to these drugs could be complicated by two main factors: costs and insurance coverage. Currently, anti-obesity drugs are not reimbursed by Swiss health insurance companies except in the presence of related diseases (diabetes, hypertension, etc.). However, this could change with the introduction of more targeted therapies.

According to IQVIA estimates, the global market for anti-obesity drugs could grow to $200 billion by 2030. Cross-border commuters, who already face high costs for cross-border healthcare, may see increased out-of-pocket expenses or have to consider specific insurance solutions.

What changes for those who already take GLP-1 drugs

Currently, popular drugs such as Wegovy and Zepbound are approved for chronic weight management in patients with BMI ≥30 or ≥27 with comorbidities. However, not everyone responds in the same way to these therapies. According to clinical studies, about 30-50% of patients do not achieve significant weight loss, and many abandon treatment due to side effects such as nausea, constipation or gastrointestinal upset.

Petrelintide, with its average weight loss of 10.7%, could represent an alternative for those who do not respond to classic GLP-1. However, the available data are still limited and it is unclear whether the drug will gain regulatory approval in the coming years. For cross-border commuters already taking therapies such as Zepbound, transitioning to a new drug could involve additional costs and bureaucratic procedures, especially if insurance coverage is not extended.

This would pave the way for a new treatment option for patients in Ticino and Lombardy, with possible repercussions on costs and accessibility. However, given the fierce competition, Roche will need to demonstrate a significant advantage over Wegovy and Zepbound.

Scenario 2: Introduction of generic drugs By 2028-2030, generic semaglutide and tirzepatide drugs could hit the market, reducing costs and increasing accessibility. This could prompt Swiss health insurers to review their reimbursement policies, including obesity as a disease to be treated. For cross-border commuters, this would mean greater access to financially viable therapies.

Scenario 3: Regulatory changes in Ticino and Lombardy The Canton of Ticino could introduce new guidelines for the management of obesity, aligning with European standards. At the same time, Lombardy could extend public health coverage for these drugs, at least in selected cases. This would require greater cooperation between the two health systems, especially for cross-border commuters who turn to both Swiss and Italian facilities.

Key points

Step 1: Assess suitability for treatment

Before considering taking anti-obesity medications, it is essential to check if you are suitable for treatment. According to the criteria of the WHO and the Swiss and Italian health authorities:

  • BMI ≥ 30: Overt obesity. - BMI ≥ 27 with comorbidities: Overweight with type 2 diabetes, high blood pressure or high cholesterol. - Age: Over 18 years old (some drugs have more restrictive limits).

For cross-border commuters living in Ticino, the assessment can be carried out at: - Endocrinology Centers of the EOC (Lugano, Bellinzona, Locarno, Mendrisio). - General practitioners with expertise in nutrition and metabolism. - Specialist clinics in Lombardy (if you opt for a cross-border approach).

If you fall within the parameters, the next step is to apply for a medical prescription, which can be used to check your insurance coverage.

Step 2: Check your insurance coverage

In Ticino, health insurers do not reimburse anti-obesity drugs except in the presence of related diseases. However, some supplemental policies may offer partial coverage. Here's what to do:

1. Contact your health insurer: Ask if it covers medications such as Wegovy or Zepbound if you have comorbidities. 2. Evaluate additional policies: Some insurance companies offer specific plans for obesity or metabolic diseases. 3. Check with your employer: Some companies offer additional benefits, such as contributions to specific therapies.

If coverage is not available, the cost of these drugs can be as high as CHF 400-500 per month in Switzerland, making them unaffordable for many. In Lombardy, costs are lower (around EUR 200-300 per month if purchased privately), but availability is limited.

Step 3: Monitor Petrelintide Updates

Petrelintide is still being studied, but preliminary results are promising. To stay up to date:

  • Follow official sources: Roche, Zealand Pharma and Swiss (FOPH) and Italian (AIFA) health authorities. - Subscribe to specialized newsletters: Sites such as Frontaliere Ticino or Swissinfo publish regular updates on drugs and regulations. - Consult your doctor: Specialists can provide up-to-date information on new treatments.

If Petrelintide obtains approval, it could be available in Ticino by 2028-2029, but it is still too early to make certain predictions.

Step 4: Evaluate cross-border alternatives

For cross-border commuters who are unable to access treatment in Switzerland, one solution could be to turn to Italy. However, even in Lombardy access is limited:

  • GLP-1 drugs: Partial reimbursement by the NHS only in severe cases (e.g., obesity with BMI >40). - Private costs: Around 200-300 EUR per month for Wegovy or Zepbound. - Specialist centres: In Milan and Monza there are clinics that follow patients with obesity, but waiting times can be long.

Before opting for a cross-border route, it is advisable to: - Check the coverage of the NHS with your general practitioner in Italy. - Contact the Ticino cantonal health service to find out if there is a collaboration with Lombardy facilities. - Consider travel and logistics costs: Frequent travel between Ticino and Lombardy could make the treatment impractical.

Step 5: Prepare for regulatory changes

The market for anti-obesity drugs is rapidly evolving, and both Switzerland and Italy could introduce new rules in the coming years. Here's what to keep an eye out for:

  • Extension of insurance coverage: Some Ticino health insurance companies may start reimbursing these drugs, especially if patient pressure increases. - New cantonal guidelines: The Ticino DSS could update its recommendations, aligning with European standards. - Cross-border collaboration: Agreements could be created between Ticino and Lombardy to facilitate access to therapies and checks.

To stay up to date, it is useful to: - Follow the official communications of the Canton of Ticino and the Lombardy Region. - Participate in information events organized by patient associations or health institutions. - Regularly consult websites such as frontaliereticino.ch or those of health insurance companies.

Frequently Asked Questions
Will Petrelintide be available in Ticino in 2026?
Not yet. The drug is still in the study phase (phase 2), and no data has been released regarding a possible approval before 2028-2029. Final clinical study results are expected in 2027.
How much does a drug like Wegovy currently cost in Ticino?
The monthly cost of Wegovy (semaglutide) in Ticino ranges between 400 and 500 CHF. This price may vary depending on the dosage and the pharmacy. Insurance coverage is only guaranteed in the presence of related conditions such as diabetes or hypertension.
Can I get a refund for an anti-obesity drug if I work in Lombardy but live in Ticino?
It depends on your health insurance and related conditions. Currently, anti-obesity drugs are not reimbursed for isolated obesity in Ticino. However, some supplementary policies or specific agreements may offer partial coverage. It is advisable to contact your insurance provider directly.
How can I find out if I am eligible for GLP-1 drug treatment?
Eligibility is based on Body Mass Index (BMI) and the presence of related conditions. For a precise calculation, you can consult your general practitioner or an endocrinology center in Ticino (EOC) or Lombardy (public/private hospitals).
Are there alternatives to injectable drugs for obesity?
Currently, the most effective drugs are GLP-1-based and tirzepatide, which require weekly injections. Alternatives include bariatric surgery (recommended for BMI >40) or behavioral therapies, but these are not drugs. Some companies are developing oral formulations, but these are not yet available.
