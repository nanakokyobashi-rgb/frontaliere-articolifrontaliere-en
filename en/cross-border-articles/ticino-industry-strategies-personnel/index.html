Over the last eighteen months, in Ticino, collective layoffs in the industrial sector have been numerous and have fueled concern about the future of the sector. The analysis, however, offers a less straightforward reading: the origin of the situation is probably to be found in management’s strategic choices rather than in economic conditions.

The account begins with the warnings circulating in recent months. The media buzz associated the industry’s difficulties with the announcement of US tariffs, the strength of the Swiss franc, the increase in energy and transport costs caused by wars. The text specifies that the tariffs actually affected a very limited proportion of Swiss companies. The factors mentioned in the interview with Stefano Modenini also include the automotive crisis and the interruption of some transport routes.

Orders and layoffs: two signals together

Modenini, director of the Associazione industrie ticinesi, describes a situation that is less concerning today than it seemed at the beginning of the year. A large proportion of companies, he reports, have a satisfactory order book for the coming months. Stability, however, does not amount to a guarantee of continuity: the wording used in the interview brings the two elements together without erasing the uncertainties.

Over the last year and a half, Modenini has followed fifteen collective layoffs. Half were linked to the industrial sector, while the others concerned the services sector. The figure describes the events he followed and falls within the same time frame as the eighteen months mentioned at the beginning. The account of the layoffs, therefore, coexists with an order book that, for most companies, appears satisfactory in the short term.

The issue involves two parties: Modenini offers the employers’ perspective, while Daniele Duma, head of GF’s melting department in Novazzano, describes the reality of his own company from the workers’ side. The future of Ticino’s industry is therefore examined both through business decisions and through the experience gained in production departments.

Operational details

Strategy matters as much as economic conditions

The central point of the in-depth analysis is the distinction between what happens in the short term and the choices that guide a company for the following years. A collective redundancy, Modenini explains, does not necessarily depend on a cyclical or economic crisis. A company may decide to diversify production or give preference to other production sites. In both cases, the region is compared with other regions.

The region as an industrial parameter

The discussion of framework conditions makes the relationship between the company and the region concrete. Modenini cites legal stability, taxation, costs and the availability of labor. Improving these conditions, according to the interview, means reducing the risk that a company will leave the region. The assessment of attractiveness therefore concerns not only the immediate cost, but also the possibility of organizing operations in subsequent years.

The same reasoning applies to innovation. The Swiss industrial sector is recognized worldwide for its technical quality and innovative capacity, but introducing innovation requires investments in tools, training and process updates. The source describes companies at different stages: some still have to begin digitization, while others are integrating artificial intelligence.

As for public instruments, the Innovation Act provides funding, but the interview points to the need to do something different and to put in place targeted instruments for small and medium-sized enterprises. Collaboration with academic partners, such as SUPSI, is instead described as positive. The framework thus combines technical quality, vocational training, research and process updates: these are the elements that the source places alongside management's strategic choices.

Labour and the border: the documented scope

The staff shortage links the issue of cross-border workers to the difficulties facing industry. The labour shortage is identified as one of the sector's main challenges and, especially in Ticino, the problem is exacerbated by the introduction of the tax agreement with neighbouring Italy that governs the taxation of cross-border workers. Modenini is also asked to consider the relationship with the demographic crisis and the agreement's possible deterrent effect on higher-income workers interested in employment in Ticino.

The reported response begins with a figure in the statistics on cross-border workers: over two years, approximately 900 female and male workers were lost, partly for age-related reasons. The date of the agreement and tax treatment

To avoid confusion over the timeline, the separately verified reference, distinct from the source, corrects the wording “new 2026 tax agreement”. The New Agreement on Cross-Border Workers was signed on 23 December 2020 and has been in force since 1 January 2024; Italy's ratification is Law 83 of 13 June 2023. The same reference specifies that withholding tax on the employment income of cross-border workers is collected only in Switzerland and that Italy avoids double taxation through the tax credit in the CE section of Form 730.
