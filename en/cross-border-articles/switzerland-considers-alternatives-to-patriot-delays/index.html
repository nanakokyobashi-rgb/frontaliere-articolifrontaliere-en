Patriot delayed, Bern seeks alternatives

Di Laura Bianchi · Published on 2 May 2026

Switzerland looks for alternatives to delayed Patriot systems, consulting Germany, France, Israel, and South Korea.

Context

In brief

  • The CHF is the world's premier safe-haven currency: neutrality, fiscal discipline, independent central bank
  • From 2007 to today, the franc has appreciated over 35% against the euro
  • On a CHF 6,000 salary, a 5% exchange-rate move is worth roughly EUR 285 per month

In May 2026, Armasuisse — Switzerland's federal defence procurement office — announced it was evaluating alternatives to the delayed US Patriot system, whose delivery has slipped by four to five years. A defence story, yes — but one that reveals something fundamental about how Switzerland works: the country does not take on emergency debt, it plans multi-year solutions within its existing budget, and it keeps public expenditure under strict constitutional control. This financial discipline is precisely the same reason the Swiss franc is worth significantly more than the euro today, and why earning in CHF gives cross-border workers a structural advantage.

For frontalieri, this dynamic is not abstract. The salary arrives in CHF, daily expenses are paid in EUR, and every exchange-rate movement directly translates into purchasing power gained or lost. Understanding why the franc is strong — and how to manage this volatility — is basic financial literacy for anyone working in Ticino.

Why the CHF is structurally strong

Four factors explain the franc's strength:

1. Schuldenbremse (debt brake): Switzerland's constitution has required a structurally balanced federal budget since 2003. Swiss public debt stands at around 27% of GDP — roughly half of Italy's. When markets become turbulent, investors move into low-debt currencies.

2. SNB independence and gold reserves: The Swiss National Bank holds over 1,000 tonnes of gold. Its interventions have historically been disciplined and targeted, not emergency money-printing.

3. Geopolitical neutrality: Switzerland's country risk is among the lowest in the world. In periods of international tension — conflicts, recessions, banking crises — global capital flows into the CHF as a refuge, increasing demand and pushing the rate upward.

4. Structurally low inflation: Switzerland has historically lower inflation than the European average. A low-inflation currency is a currency that holds its value over time.

Operational details

The CHF-EUR exchange rate: twenty years of history every frontaliere should know

In 2007, one euro bought roughly 1.68 Swiss francs. In January 2015, the SNB scrapped the 1.20 floor it had set in 2011 — and within minutes the franc appreciated 15–20%, one of the most violent single-day moves in modern currency history. In 2026 the rate settles around 0.93–0.95 CHF per euro.

The numbers: from 1.68 to 0.93 means that someone earning CHF 6,000 per month today receives approximately EUR 5,580, compared with EUR 3,571 they would have received in 2007 for the same CHF amount. The franc has strengthened by over 35% in real terms against the euro over twenty years.

This does not mean the CHF always rises. It stabilised in 2017–2019. In 2022–2023, aggressive ECB rate hikes temporarily narrowed the gap. But the structural trend — CHF stronger — is confirmed by all the fundamental indicators.

The concrete impact on a frontaliere's salary

Take a typical frontaliere: gross salary CHF 6,500, net after withholding tax roughly CHF 5,800. Converted to euros:

  • At 0.95: EUR 5,510
  • At 0.90: EUR 5,220
  • At 0.85: EUR 4,930

The difference between the current rate and a 10% adverse-rate scenario is roughly EUR 580 per month — more than EUR 6,900 per year. An amount that easily justifies spending time optimising the conversion.

Practical conversion scenarios

Scenario 1 — Classic bank transfer: Italian banks apply a worse-than-interbank exchange rate (typically 1.5–2.5% spread) plus fixed fees. On CHF 5,800, the hidden cost can reach EUR 80–140 per month — up to EUR 1,680 per year in undisclosed bank spread.

Scenario 2 — Wise or Revolut: Fintech services use the mid-market rate with transparent fees (0.35–0.7%). On CHF 5,800 the fee is roughly EUR 25–40. Saving vs. bank transfer: EUR 50–100 per month, or EUR 600–1,200 per year.

Scenario 3 — Mixed strategy with timing: Keep one to two months' salary in CHF in a Swiss or multi-currency account. Convert only when the rate is above the 30-day average, using automatic rate alerts on fintech apps. This does not guarantee gains, but reduces the risk of systematically converting at unfavourable moments.

Does the timing of conversion matter?

Currency markets open on Monday mornings with wider spreads (bank desks reopen after the weekend with less liquidity). Thursday and Friday mornings typically offer tighter spreads. This is not speculation — it is simply choosing the least expensive moment for a routine transaction.

Key points

Opening a multi-currency account in Italy: is it possible?

Yes. Several banks and payment institutions in Italy offer current accounts with an Italian IBAN that allow you to receive and hold balances in CHF, EUR and other currencies.

  • Revolut with Italian IBAN: receive in CHF, convert when you choose at the market rate. Note: SEPA and SWIFT payments can sometimes be delayed
  • Wise multi-currency account: Italian local IBAN, separate CHF balance. Excellent for receiving direct Swiss salary transfers
  • Traditional bank with a CHF account abroad: some branches of Crédit Agricole and UBS Italia offer foreign-currency accounts for Italian residents with cross-border employment. Higher but more predictable costs

Tax note: a foreign bank account (including a Swiss one) must be declared in Schedule RW of the Italian personal income tax return if the balance exceeds EUR 5,000 at any point during the year. This is not an additional tax on the balance — it is a monitoring obligation. Failure to declare carries penalties proportional to the undeclared amount.

Frequently Asked Questions
Why is the Swiss franc so strong against the euro?
The CHF is structurally strong for four main reasons: the constitutional debt brake (Schuldenbremse) that keeps Swiss public debt at around 27% of GDP, SNB gold reserves of over 1,000 tonnes, Swiss neutrality that attracts global capital in periods of tension, and historically low inflation. These factors combined mean that in periods of global uncertainty, capital flows into CHF, strengthening it further.
How much do I save using Wise instead of a bank transfer to convert my CHF salary to euros?
On a net salary of CHF 5,800 per month, the typical saving is EUR 50–100 per month compared with a classic bank transfer, which applies an exchange spread of 1.5–2.5% that is not always explicitly disclosed. Over a year the saving can reach EUR 600–1,200. Wise and Revolut use the mid-market rate with transparent fees in the range of 0.35–0.7%.
Do I need to declare my Swiss bank account to the Italian tax authorities?
Yes, if the balance exceeds EUR 5,000 at any point during the year. It must be declared in Schedule RW of the Italian personal income tax return (Redditi PF). This is not an additional tax on the balance but a tax monitoring obligation. Failure to declare is subject to penalties calculated as a percentage of the undeclared value. Even modest accounts used only for daily Swiss work expenses must be declared.
