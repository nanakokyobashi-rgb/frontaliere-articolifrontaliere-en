Devastate a car and insult passengers: 34-year-old stopped on the train (cross-border guide)

A 34-year-old Italian, impaired by alcohol, devastated a wagon on the Como-lago line, tearing up signs and breaking fire extinguishers. Stopped by the State Police.

In brief

  • 34-year-old stopped at Como-lago after trashing a train car
  • Internal signage torn off and fire extinguisher seals broken
  • Under the influence of alcohol, without documents
  • Reported for aggravated damage and interruption of public service

Key facts

  • What: trashing of a train car and insults to passengers
  • When: yesterday afternoon, around 5:00 PM
  • Where: Como-lago railway station
  • Who: Italian citizen, 34 years old, resident in Lezzeno (Como)
  • Charges: aggravated damage, interruption of public service

The typical day for those who take the train to work in Switzerland involves delays, a few bumps, and maybe some noisy passage. Certainly not a trashed train car. Yet that is what happened yesterday afternoon aboard a train arriving at Como-lago station, where officers from a State Police patrol intervened to stop a man who was causing chaos.

According to what has been reconstructed, around 5:00 PM a 34-year-old Italian citizen, resident in the nearby Lezzeno, was identified and stopped. The man was under the influence of alcohol, had no documents with him, and was aggressive toward passengers, some of whom received insulting remarks. But he did not limit himself to words: he tore off the internal signage of the train car and broke the seals on the fire extinguishers, making police intervention necessary.

The 34-year-old is no stranger to such incidents: he has quite a few prior records. After being stopped, he was reported to the judicial authority on charges of aggravated damage and interruption of public service, since his behavior caused the train to be blocked.

Operational details

Analysis: when chaos on the train becomes a problem for the cross-border commuter

A news event like the one at Como-lago may seem far from the routine of those working in Ticino. But anyone who takes the train to Chiasso or Lugano every day knows that a single out-of-service carriage can mean delays for hundreds of people. And delays, for those with a G permit and a schedule to keep, are never trivial.

The impact on commuters

The Como-Lugano line is one of the main arteries for Italian cross-border workers employed in Canton Ticino. Every morning, thousands of people cross the border at Chiasso or Brogeda, and many do so by train. A disruption like yesterday afternoon's, although limited in duration, can create a domino effect: missed connections, late arrivals, and the need to notify the employer.

For those with a G permit, the delay is not just a matter of punctuality. In some cases, Swiss employment contracts provide limited tolerance for lateness, and cross-border workers often have to justify every minute of absence. This is not a bureaucratic issue: it is the daily reality for those working across the border.

What to do in case of disruption

If you find yourself in a similar situation, the first thing is to stay calm and report the incident to on-board staff or the police. In cases of damage, like yesterday's, safety is the priority: do not approach broken fire extinguishers or hazardous signage.

For delays, it is advisable to document everything: departure time, actual arrival, and, if possible, a photo of the blocked train. This can be used to justify a delay at work or for any refund requests, if the service provider offers compensation for disruptions.

Key points

How to behave in emergency situations on the train

When an episode like the one in Como-lago occurs, the most important thing is to know how to react. Here is a practical three-step procedure.

1. Assess the situation

If you notice an aggressive person or ongoing damage, do not intervene directly. Keep your distance and immediately notify onboard staff or, if possible, call the police. In yesterday's case, the intervention of a patrol car was necessary to stop the man, who was in a state of alcohol-induced alteration.

2. Document the disruptions

If the train stops due to an incident, note the exact time and, if you can, take a photo of the damaged carriage. This information can be useful for:

  • Justifying a delay to your employer
  • Requesting a possible refund from the service operator
  • Reporting the episode to the competent authorities

3. Protect your rights

For those working in Switzerland with a G permit, delays can have practical consequences. In case of prolonged disruptions, check with your employer whether it is possible to make up the hours or if there are specific agreements for cross-border commuters. Transparency is essential: a documented delay is very different from an unjustified absence.
