Travelling accident at the border: LAINF coverage (cross-border guide)

Di Redazione Frontaliere Ticino · Published on 27 August 2026 · Updated on 25 September 2026

You are covered by LAINF insurance during the commute between Italy and Switzerland. Practical Guide to Coverage, Complaint Procedures, and Deadlines.

Context

In a nutshell

  • LAINF covers accidents during the commute from Italy to Switzerland
  • Direct route covered; detours and alternative routes excluded from coverage
  • Reporting must take place within the deadlines provided to the insurer by the employer

Key facts

  • What: Accident during the usual home-work route
  • Who: Frontier workers (G permit) employed in Switzerland
  • Where: Border routes (Brogeda, Chiasso, Gaggiolo, Ponte Tresa) and Ticino road
  • Coverage: LAINF – Accident Insurance Law (0.7–1.5% contribution)
  • Protection: Medical expenses, hospital expenses, compensation for incapacity for work, pension in case of disability

Working in Switzerland as frontaliere con permesso G means being automatically covered by LAINF (Accident Insurance Act) insurance. It is not a choice: it is mandatory by Swiss federal law. Your employer pays the contribution (0.7–1.5%) and you are enrolled. An accident that occurs while commuting from home (in Italy) to the workplace (in Switzerland) is covered by this coverage.

LAINF stands for the Swiss Federal Accident Insurance Act. It is mandatory for all employees in Switzerland, including border workers with a G permit. Operational details

The daily reality of a cross-border worker (frontaliero) is a continuous commute: you leave home, cross the border (Brogeda, Chiasso, Gaggiolo, Ponte Tresa), reach your company, and return. Throughout this entire journey, you are 'under protection' under LAINF. But what does this mean in practice?

Imagine working at a Ticino-based company (for example, in Mendrisio, Lugano, or Bellinzona). Every morning you cover the same route: perhaps 30–50 km from your home to the border and then on to the company. This is your 'usual commute'. If one day a car hits you at the Chiasso border crossing and you are injured, the LAINF insurer pays for everything: ambulance, hospitalization, medical care. If you cannot work for 2 months because of the accident, you receive an indemnity (usually a percentage of your salary) for that period. If the accident leaves permanent scars or reduces your working capacity, you receive a pension. You do not have to pay anything out of your pocket for these benefits.

This works because your employer has paid the LAINF contribution (between 0.7% and 1.5% of your annual salary) to a specific insurer (often SUVA, but also other entities designated by the employer). That contribution covers accident risks. For the cross-border worker, this is doubly important: workplace risks plus the risks of the cross-border commute.

Standard commute vs. business trip: what changes

Here an important distinction comes into play. LAINF covers the 'usual' home-to-work commute. But what happens if your employer sends you on a business trip to another city (for example, a meeting at another office in Switzerland, or a company event)? Or if your boss asks you to deliver goods to a client?

If you are on a business trip (meaning your employer has tasked you with going somewhere other than your usual office), you are still covered by LAINF. However, the coverage is different: it is no longer 'in itinere' (home-to-work commute), but a 'professional' accident (arising from your work activity). Practically speaking, you are covered in the same way, but the administrative category is different. If you are injured while on a business trip for work, LAINF still pays.

Check your contributions on your payslip to make sure your employer has correctly paid the LAINF contribution. The problem arises if you decide to make a voluntary deviation. Imagine: you should go directly to the company, but you decide to stop by a friend's place for breakfast. Or you decide to take a completely different route out of curiosity. In that case, if you are injured during the deviation, there may be a reduction in coverage or an exclusion (depending on how significant the deviation is and the insurer's assessment).

Cross-border workers and border crossings: a specific risk situation

The cross-border worker who crosses the Brogeda, Chiasso, Gaggiolo, or Ponte Tresa checkpoints daily has a slightly higher risk exposure than a Swiss worker who reaches the company 5 km away. Cross-border traffic is heavy, especially during peak hours (morning 7–9 am, evening 5–7 pm). Queues at the border crossings are frequent and can cause stress, rushing, and risky behaviors (sudden lane changes, excessive speed, distraction).

Under Swiss LAINF law, there is no special 'subcategory' for cross-border workers facing additional risks. You are covered in the same way: if an accident occurs during the commute, it is a commuting accident (in itinere) and LAINF steps in. Your entitlement is identical to that of any other employee in Switzerland.

Key points

If you are injured during your commute to and from work, act quickly. The procedure is simple, but timing matters.

First step: assess the situation and seek medical help

If the injury is serious (car accident, major trauma), call 112 (or the local Swiss emergency number 144 for an ambulance). Do not move the body if there are significant injuries. If the injury is minor (small fall, light bruising), you can go directly to the emergency room or to your general practitioner.

Second step: gather evidence and details

If you were in a car accident, make sure a police report is filed. Take photos of the scene, collect the details of any witnesses (names, phone numbers). Note the date, time, exact location, and what happened. If you received medical care, keep all documents: medical reports, prescriptions, medical certificates.

Third step: notify your employer

Inform your employer of the injury as soon as possible. The law does not set a specific deadline, but best practice is to notify them within the same day or the following day. The employer is required to report the injury to the LAINF insurer (usually SUVA or another body) within a certain period set by the insurance contract and Swiss law.

Fourth step: fill in the claim form

The LAINF insurer (through the employer) will provide an official claim form. This form requires: date and time of the injury, exact location, detailed circumstances, any witnesses, description of the injuries, and treatment received. Complete the form carefully and attach the medical documentation.

Fifth step: follow the benefits process

Once the claim has been submitted, the insurer examines the case. If it is recognized as a commuting accident (in itinere) covered by LAINF, the insurer begins to cover the medical costs. If you remain in hospital, the insurer coordinates directly with the hospital facility in Ticino or in the canton where you are receiving care. Days of incapacity are compensated starting from the first day (or sometimes from the third, depending on the policy). If you remain unable to work for a long time, you can check your unemployment rights and benefits.

Important timelines and deadlines

There is no rigid nationwide 'deadline schedule' for reporting a commuting accident. However, there is a strong recommendation to act within a few days. If you wait weeks, it may become difficult to gather evidence, witnesses may no longer remember the details, and doctors may not link later treatment back to the original injury.

If the insurer denies coverage (for example, claiming that it was not a regular commute or that there was a voluntary deviation), you have the right to file an appeal. Appeal deadlines are governed by Swiss legislation and depend on the canton. It is advisable to consult a legal advisor specialized in Swiss labor law if the appeal is complex.

Where to turn and what you need

For tax and contribution matters, your employer deals with SECO (State Secretariat for Economic Affairs) or the relevant cantonal bodies. For the specific LAINF insurance, responsibility lies with the chosen insurer (often SUVA). If you reside in Italy and work in Switzerland (G permit), the Italian INPS does not intervene on Swiss accidents: it is the exclusive competence of Switzerland.

Practical tip: Before you find yourself in this situation, ask your employer which LAINF insurer is assigned to your company and how to contact them in case of injury. Having the phone number at hand (perhaps saved in your phone) can make a difference when you need to act quickly.

Frequently Asked Questions
What exactly does LAINF cover for a border worker working in Switzerland?
LAINF covers accidents during the work-home journey (on-the-job accident), accidents at work in the company, and occupational diseases caused by the type of activity. Benefits include: medical and hospital expenses, daily allowance for incapacity for work (usually a percentage of salary), and annuity in case of permanent disability. For the border worker with a G permit, the coverage is identical to that of a Swiss worker: the mandatory contribution (0.7–1.5%) is decided by the employer at the t
If I get injured while driving to work at border crossings (Brogeda, Chiasso), am I covered?
Yes, if you have been involved in a car accident during your usual commute, LAINF automatically covers you. The passes of Brogeda, Chiasso, Gaggiolo and Ponte Tresa are part of the direct route between the house and the company for many border crossers. Even if you get stuck in traffic and a wreck happens, the fact that you stayed in the usual path means you stay covered. The insurer will not reduce coverage because you were at the crossings or because the traffic was heavy.
If I make a stop (e.g., pharmacy, bank) on the way to work, do I stay covered?
A short stop for a need (pharmacy, gas, package pickup) may not exclude coverage if it remains part of the reasonably necessary journey. However, a significant deviation (for example, going shopping, visiting a friend off the beaten path) could reduce or exclude coverage. The insurer will evaluate the concrete case. If you're in doubt, it's best to avoid detours during your daily commute.
Within how long do I have to report an on-line accident to the LAINF insurer?
There is no strict federal law deadline, but it is strongly advised to report within a few days (ideally the same day or the next day). Your employer has a duty to notify the insurer of the accident within the terms of the insurance contract (usually 3–10 days). The longer you wait, the harder it will be to gather evidence and testimony. If you delay for more than a week, you may complicate the investigation.
If the insurer denies coverage, what can I do?
If LAINF denies coverage (stating for example that it was not a usual route), you have the right to lodge an appeal according to Swiss procedures. The canton of competence is the one where the accident occurred or where the company resides. Administrative and judicial appeals are possible, and it is advisable to consult a lawyer specialising in Swiss labour law if the case is complex or the amount is significant.
