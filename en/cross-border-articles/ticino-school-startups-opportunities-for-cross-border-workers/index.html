Key Facts

  • What: Project My business, my future promoted by the Sodalitas Foundation, which generated over 500 business ideas from Lombardy students.
  • When: Edition ended in 2026 (fifth edition).
  • Where: Provinces of Varese, Milan, Monza, Sondrio, Genoa and Turin, with activities at the Ville Ponti in Varese.

  • Amount: No specific financial data in the source, but involvement of 3,000 students and 100+ seminars.
  • Method: Learning by doing with concrete project work and business plans.
  • Focus: Sustainability, social innovation and green/digital transition.

The fifth edition of the project My business, my future, promoted by the Sodalitas Foundation, has transformed thousands of Lombard students into aspiring entrepreneurs. The initiative, which ended in Varese, involved about 3,000 students from 28 high schools and, for the first time, from 4 ITS in the provinces of Varese, Milan, Monza, Sondrio, Genoa and Turin. The result? Over 500 business ideas focused on sustainability and social innovation, the result of a path that has united school desks and the corporate world.

An experiential training model

The project did not limit itself to theory, but adopted a learning by doing approach. Through over 100 seminars and project work activities, students worked in teams to develop concrete business models and business plans. They were led by volunteer managers from organisations such as Brianza Solidale and VSP – Senior Professional Volunteers, who made their experience available to face the challenges of the green and digital transition.

The novelty this year was the inclusion of the Higher Technical Institutes (ITS) in the training course. According to Alberto Pirelli, President of the Sodalitas Foundation, this choice reflects the desire to "embrace the entire training chain" to cultivate the talent of the new generations through concrete experiences. An approach that, according to the president, is essential to build the human capital necessary for companies to remain competitive.

Implications for Ticino and cross-border commuters

The Lombard project, although not aimed at cross-border workers, offers interesting ideas for the Canton of Ticino, where training and innovation are key to attracting and retaining cross-border workers. The Chamber of Commerce of Varese has underlined how investing in young people is strategic to ensure competitiveness in the territory, a principle that could also be extended to Ticino, where the demand for specialized skills is growing.

In particular, the laboratory teaching model adopted in the Lombard project could inspire Ticino schools to go beyond traditional face-to-face lessons, training skills such as creativity, ethical thinking and teamwork. An approach that, according to Giuseppe Carcano, Director of the Varese Territorial School Office, responds to the needs of students to "plan their future proactively".

Focus on sustainability and innovation The 500 business ideas developed by the Lombard students focus on two central themes: sustainability and social innovation. These are sectors in which Ticino is also investing, with projects such as the energy transition and the circular economy. For cross-border commuters working in Swiss companies active in these sectors, these skills could represent added value in the labour market.

Crucial question for cross-border commuters If the Lombard model shows that experiential training can generate innovative ideas, why not replicate it in Ticino? For example, collaborations between Ticino high schools (such as the Liceo di Lugano or the Istituto Cantonale di Mendrisio) and local businesses could create opportunities for cross-border commuters eager to update their skills or start entrepreneurial activities. However, it should be remembered that in the Canton of Ticino the regulatory and tax context for startups is different from that in Lombardy, with specific rules on work permits, withholding tax and AVS/LPP contribution.

Operational details

How the Lombardy model can inspire us in Ticino

The My Business, My Future project shows that experiential training not only stimulates creativity, but also builds a bridge between school and the world of work. For cross‑border workers who live in Italy but work in Ticino, this model offers interesting ideas on how to update skills, start entrepreneurial activities or simply better understand the Swiss market.

1. Continuous training for cross‑border workers: opportunities and limits

In Ticino, continuous training is governed by specific regulations, especially for those holding a B permit (residence) or a G permit (cross‑border worker). While in Lombardy the project mainly targets students, in Ticino it could also interest adult employees who wish to retrain or launch a small business.

Limits to consider:

  • Work permit: A cross‑border worker with a G permit can undertake entrepreneurial activities only if they are compatible with the main contract. For more demanding activities, a B permit may be required.
  • Taxation: Income from entrepreneurial activities in Switzerland is subject to withholding tax and AVS/LPP contributions. If the activity is carried out in Italy, Italian tax rules apply, with possible double taxation to be managed through the 1976 Italy‑Switzerland Convention.
  • Time: Many cross‑border workers are full‑time employees in Switzerland. Attending courses or workshops requires careful planning, possibly using remote work or vacation periods.

2. Sustainability and innovation: key sectors for cross‑border workers

The 500 entrepreneurial ideas born in Lombardy focus on sustainability and social innovation, sectors that are also growing strongly in Ticino. Here are some concrete opportunities for cross‑border workers:

A. Energy and green transition

Ticino is investing in the energy transition, with projects such as renewable energy use and sustainable mobility. A cross‑border worker in the energy sector could:

  • Attend energy‑efficiency courses organized by bodies like SUPSI or the Canton of Ticino.
  • Launch a small energy‑consulting business for private clients or SMEs, leveraging skills acquired in Switzerland.
  • Collaborate with social cooperatives operating in the sustainability field.

Note: If the activity involves hiring employees, Swiss rules on cross‑border work and AVS/LPP contributions for collaborators must be checked.

B. Social innovation and the elderly

Ticino's population is aging, creating many opportunities in personal services, especially for those working in the social or health sectors. A concrete example:

  • A cross‑border nurse could certify additional skills in home care or elderly‑facility management.
  • A tourism‑sector employee could start a sustainable tour‑operator business, promoting eco‑friendly itineraries in Ticino.

Reference regulations:

  • G permit: Allows secondary activities only if they do not prejudice the main job.
  • B permit: Allows opening full‑time entrepreneurial activities, but with Swiss tax obligations.

3. Collaborations between schools and businesses: a model to replicate?

The Chamber of Commerce of Varese has highlighted how the Lombardy project is a strategy to retain talent in the region. In Ticino, where the brain drain to other Swiss regions is a known phenomenon, similar initiatives could be useful.

Examples of possible collaborations:

  • Ticino high schools (such as the Liceo di Lugano or the Istituto Cantonale di Bellinzona) could partner with local SMEs for entrepreneurship workshops.
  • Cross-border workers with managerial experience could become mentors for students or young entrepreneurs.
  • Institutions like SUPSI could organize specific courses for cross-border workers on Swiss tax regulations and self-employment opportunities.

Barriers to overcome:

  • Language: Many courses in Ticino are in Italian or German. An Italian cross-border worker might struggle if they don’t speak German.
  • Time: Most cross-border workers work full-time. Evening or weekend courses could be the solution.
  • Costs: Some training programs come at a cost. Are there cantonal subsidies or scholarships available for cross-border workers?

⚠️ Watch out for tax pitfalls

A common mistake among cross-border workers starting businesses is underestimating tax obligations. Here's what to keep in mind:

  • Double taxation: If the business generates income in both countries, you must file tax returns in Italy and pay withholding tax in Switzerland. The 1976 Convention avoids double taxation, but you need to fill out the forms correctly.
  • AVS/LPP: Income from business activities is also subject to Swiss social security contributions if the business operates in Switzerland.
  • VAT number: If the business exceeds a certain income threshold, you may need to register for a VAT number in Italy.

Frequently Asked Questions
Can I start a business venture with a **G permit** in Ticino?
Yes, but only if the activity is **secondary** and does not interfere with your main job. If the activity requires more time or resources, you may need to switch to a **B permit**. Always check with the **Cantonal Employment Office** or your employer before proceeding.
What are the tax obligations for running a business in Ticino?
If your activity is based in Switzerland, your income is subject to **Swiss withholding tax** and **AVS/LPP contributions**. If you also generate income in Italy, you’ll need to declare it in both countries, applying the **1976 Italy-Switzerland tax treaty** to avoid double taxation. Consult a specialized accountant for guidance.
Where can I find training courses for startups in Ticino?
**SUPSI** and the **Ticino Chamber of Commerce** offer courses and workshops on entrepreneurship, sustainability, and business planning. Some are also aimed at cross-border workers. Check availability on [supsi.ch/continua](https://www.supsi.ch/en/continua) or [ti.ch/commerce](https://www.ti.ch/en/canton/institutions/chamber-of-commerce/).
Are there funding opportunities for startups in Ticino?
Yes, the **Canton of Ticino** provides grants for innovative projects, particularly in **green tech** and **social sectors**. Additionally, banks like **BancaStato** or **Raiffeisen** offer favorable loans. Contact the **Cantonal Office of Economy and Health** for detailed information.
Can I register a VAT number in Switzerland if I live in Italy?
It depends on your activity. If your business operates mainly in Switzerland and exceeds **CHF 100,000 in annual revenue**, you’ll need to register for VAT with the **Cantonal Tax Office**. If the activity is based in Italy, you must register in your country of residence. Consult an accountant to assess your specific case.
